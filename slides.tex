\begin{frame}{What's in the Repository}
  \small
  \textbf{Library} (\texttt{src/powerflow/}, $\sim$4000 lines): one shared
  \texttt{PowerCase} model and $Y_{bus}$ builder feed \textbf{seven solvers}
  behind a single \texttt{solve(case, options, ybus)} interface:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SNR} --- standard Newton--Raphson, power mismatch (baseline)
    \item \textbf{PNR} --- simplified NR, current mismatch (\emph{the base paper})
    \item \textbf{PNR+} --- our extension: PV reactive power as a genuine Newton unknown
    \item \textbf{RCI} --- rectangular current injection (project proposal's formulation)
    \item \textbf{FDLF} --- fast decoupled load flow, XB and BX variants
    \item \textbf{GS} --- Gauss--Seidel, classical baseline
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Driven by} \texttt{run\_all.py} over \textbf{9 test systems}
  (paper's 3-bus example, IEEE 5/6/24/30/57/118/300-bus) $\to$
  \texttt{results/tables/} (CSV) and \texttt{results/figures/} (30 PNGs).
  \textbf{Verified by} \texttt{pytest}: \textbf{251 tests, all passing} ---
  every solver checked against a $10^{-12}$ p.u.\ NR reference on every case.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Problem Statement}
  \small
  \textbf{Power flow:} given a network of $n$ nodes (buses) and known power
  injections, find the unknown voltage at every node.

  \medskip
  \begin{itemize}
    \item This is an $n$-dimensional \textbf{root-finding problem}: $n$
      nonlinear equations in $n$ unknown complex voltages.
    \item Solved by \textbf{Newton--Raphson}: build a Jacobian, solve
      $J\,\Delta x = -f(x)$, update, repeat.
    \item \textbf{Bottleneck:} rebuilding $J$ every iteration costs
      $O(n^2)$ for a dense network.
  \end{itemize}

  \medskip
  \textbf{Base paper's claim:} rewrite the equations in an equivalent form
  whose Jacobian is cheaper to build --- same root, same convergence,
  less work per iteration.
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Two Formulations}
  \small
  \textbf{Standard NR} --- power mismatch equations:
  \[
    \Delta P_k,\ \Delta Q_k \;=\; \text{function of }
    |V_k|\,|V_i|\,|Y_{ki}|\,\cos/\sin(\theta_{ki}+\delta_i-\delta_k)
  \]

  \textbf{Simplified NR (the paper)} --- current mismatch equations:
  \[
    G_k,\ H_k \;=\; \text{function of }
    |V_i|\,|Y_{ki}|\,\cos/\sin(\theta_{ki}+\delta_i)
  \]

  \medskip
  \textbf{Same root} (multiplying a nonlinear equation by a nonzero factor
  doesn't move its zero), but the current-mismatch summand:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item drops the $|V_k|$ factor,
    \item drops $-\delta_k$ from the angle,
  \end{itemize}
  so each Jacobian entry needs \emph{fewer multiplications}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Finding 1: The Complexity Claim Is Wrong}
  \small
  \textbf{Paper claims:} Jacobian cost drops from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n)$.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Why that can't be right:} a dense Jacobian has $O(n^2)$ \emph{distinct}
  entries --- filling $n^2$ distinct numbers needs at least $O(n^2)$ work,
  not $O(n)$. The paper's own Table 1 counts off-diagonal work once per
  \emph{row}, not per \emph{entry}.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Recounting by hand, entry by entry} (same reuse of common
  subexpressions for both methods):
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Method & per off-diag.\ pair & per bus (diagonal) & per mismatch term \\
    \midrule
    SNR & 9 & 4  & 4 \\
    PNR & 5 & 13 & 3 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  Both methods are $O(n^2)$; the dense asymptotic ratio is a
  \textbf{constant} $9/5=1.80$. At the paper's own 57-bus case, this dense
  model predicts a speedup of \textbf{1.733$\times$} --- the paper's own
  \emph{measured} speedup there is \textbf{1.728$\times$}. The conclusion
  survives; the stated growth rate does not.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Finding 1b: Real Networks Are Sparse}
  \small
  The dense model above assumes every bus connects to every other bus.
  Real transmission grids don't: each bus has only $d\approx3$ neighbours
  on average, so the predicted ratio becomes
  $r(d) = \dfrac{9d+4}{5d+13}$ ---
  crossing 1 at $d=2.25$, reaching the dense asymptote $1.80$ only as
  $d\to\infty$.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \begin{center}
      \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
        \toprule
        Case & $n$ & avg.\ $d$ & Sparse ratio \\
        \midrule
        case57\_ieee  & 57  & 2.74 & 1.16 \\
        case118\_ieee & 118 & 3.03 & 1.18 \\
        case300\_ieee & 300 & 2.73 & 1.15 \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}
      \end{center}
      \vspace{4pt}
      \textbf{Real per-iteration saving: only $\sim$15--18\%}, not the
      order-of-magnitude the paper's Table 1 implies.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{results/figures/02_ratio_vs_degree.png}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Finding 2: Scale Testing Reveals a Hidden Cost}
  \small
  The paper only tested up to 57 buses. We tested up to \textbf{300 buses}.

  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Buses & SNR (standard) & PNR (paper) & PNR+ (our fix) \\
    \midrule
    5   & 4  & 4  & 4 \\
    118 & 4  & \textbf{12} & 5 \\
    300 & 5  & \textbf{13} & 6 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \textbf{Cause:} the paper \emph{approximates} one unknown at PV buses
  (reactive power $Q$) using last iteration's value, instead of solving for
  it. This breaks quadratic convergence at those buses --- and larger grids
  have more PV buses.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Our fix (PNR+):} treat $Q$ as a real Newton unknown $\Rightarrow$
  quadratic convergence restored, iteration count stays close to standard NR.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Extensions: Method Comparison, Memory, Robustness}
  \small
  \textbf{vs.\ Fast Decoupled (FDLF) and Gauss--Seidel} (300-bus system,
  \texttt{05\_method\_comparison.csv}):
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Method & Iterations & Min.\ solve time & Convergence \\
    \midrule
    SNR      & 5    & 13.6~ms   & quadratic \\
    PNR      & 13   & 32.8~ms   & linear (PV buses) \\
    FDLF-XB  & 11   & 2.9~ms    & linear (cheapest/iter) \\
    Gauss--Seidel & 3000 (did not converge) & 3.1~s & linear, too slow \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Memory:} SNR and PNR need essentially the \emph{same}
      peak memory at fixed $n$ (e.g.\ 1.74 vs.\ 1.76~MB at 118 buses) ---
      the speedup is a \emph{time} optimization, not a space one.
    \item \textbf{Robustness:} on the 118-bus system, PNR loses convergence
      earlier under stress --- \textbf{1.6$\times$} nominal load vs.\
      SNR/RCI's \textbf{3.0$\times$} --- largely recovered by PNR+
      (\textbf{2.0$\times$}).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conclusion}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The paper's \textbf{core idea is correct}: reformulating power
      mismatch as current mismatch gives a genuinely cheaper Jacobian.
    \item Its \textbf{complexity claim is not}: it's $O(n^2)$ with a smaller
      constant, not $O(n)$ --- confirmed by re-deriving the operation count,
      and validated against the paper's own measured 57-bus speedup.
    \item On \textbf{real sparse grids} the saving is $\sim$15--18\% per
      iteration, not an order of magnitude.
    \item Its \textbf{PV-bus approximation costs more than it looks}: extra
      iterations that grow with network size, and lost robustness under
      stress --- both fixed by our extension (PNR+).
    \item Everything is reproducible: \texttt{python run\_all.py}
      regenerates every table/figure; \texttt{pytest} runs 251 verification
      tests (finite-difference checks, paper's own printed numbers, etc.).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
