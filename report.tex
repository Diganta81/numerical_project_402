\begin{abstract}
Kulworawanichpong's \emph{simplified Newton--Raphson} power-flow method replaces
the power-mismatch equations of the standard formulation with nodal
current-mismatch equations. The roots are identical, but the Jacobian entries
become far simpler expressions, so the paper claims a large reduction in
per-iteration cost. This project reimplements both formulations from first
principles, reproduces every reproducible table and figure of the base paper,
and then subjects its central claim to an independent audit. Three findings
emerge. First, the reproduction is exact where the paper is checkable: the
3-bus worked example matches the published Y-bus, Jacobian, mismatch vector and
converged solution to every printed digit. Second, the paper's complexity
analysis is wrong in an identifiable way --- its Table~1 counts off-diagonal
work once per matrix \emph{row} rather than once per \emph{entry}, which makes
the cost appear linear in the bus count. An element-by-element recount shows
both methods are quadratic, separated by a constant factor approaching $9/5$; it
predicts $1.733$ at 57 buses against the $1.728$ the paper measured, so the
conclusion survives even though the arithmetic does not. Third, end-to-end the
method is \emph{slower}: lagging PV-bus reactive power destroys quadratic
convergence and inflates the iteration count from 4 to 13 at 300 buses, and an
augmented variant making reactive power a Newton unknown restores it. Finally,
freezing and reusing the LU factorisation --- the paper's own premise carried
further --- yields a $2.85\times$ speed-up at 300 buses, where factorisation is
$67\%$ of an iteration and the Jacobian build the paper optimises only $30\%$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Base Paper Review}
%% ===================================================================

\subsection{Background}
The power-flow (load-flow) problem asks for the steady-state complex voltage at
every bus of an interconnected electrical network, given the real and reactive
power injected or withdrawn at each bus. It is the computational kernel of
essentially every power-system study --- operational planning, contingency
screening, stability assessment, electricity-market clearing --- and is solved
many thousands of times per day in control centres worldwide. Because the
network equations are nonlinear, the problem is solved iteratively, and the cost
of a single power-flow solution directly limits how many scenarios an operator
can afford to examine.

Since Tinney and Hart's formulation~\cite{tinney1967}, the Newton--Raphson
method has been the standard. It converges quadratically and typically reaches
engineering accuracy in four to six iterations regardless of network size. Its
cost per iteration, however, is dominated by two operations: rebuilding the
Jacobian matrix from the current voltage estimate, and factorising it. A long
line of research has attacked one or the other. The fast decoupled load flow of
Stott and Alsa\c{c}~\cite{stott1974} replaces the Jacobian by two \emph{constant}
matrices that are factorised once, trading Newton's convergence rate for a
near-free iteration. Current-injection formulations in rectangular
coordinates~\cite{dacosta1999} make the bulk of the Jacobian constant while
retaining Newton's rate. The base paper of this project belongs to the same
family: it leaves the Newton framework and the state variables untouched, and
attacks only the arithmetic cost of forming the Jacobian entries.

\subsection{The Base Paper}
The selected base paper is T. Kulworawanichpong, \emph{``Simplified
Newton--Raphson power-flow solution method''}, International Journal of
Electrical Power \& Energy Systems \textbf{32} (2010)
551--558~\cite{kulworawanichpong2010}.

Its proposal is compact. The standard method drives the \emph{power} mismatch
to zero,
\begin{equation}
\Delta S_k = S_{\mathrm{sch},k} - V_k \overline{\textstyle\sum_i Y_{ki} V_i} = 0 ,
\label{eq:power-mismatch}
\end{equation}
whereas the paper drives the nodal \emph{current} mismatch to zero,
\begin{equation}
F_k = \overline{\left(\frac{S_{\mathrm{sch},k}}{V_k}\right)}
      - \sum_{i=1}^{n} Y_{ki} V_i = 0 .
\label{eq:current-mismatch}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:power-mismatch} and~\eqref{eq:current-mismatch} have exactly
the same roots --- the second is the first divided by $\overline{V_k}$ --- so
nothing about the solution changes. What changes is the algebra of the
derivatives. In the standard summand
$|V_k V_i Y_{ki}|\cos(\theta_{ki} + \delta_i - \delta_k)$ both the near-end and
the far-end bus appear; in the simplified summand
$|Y_{ki}V_i|\cos(\theta_{ki} + \delta_i)$ there is no $|V_k|$ factor and no
$-\delta_k$ in the angle. Every off-diagonal Jacobian entry therefore depends on
a single bus index, and the diagonal entries become closed-form expressions
instead of row sums.

The paper quantifies the saving in its Section~3 and claims that the Jacobian
cost per iteration falls from $10n^2 + O(n)$ multiplications to $22(n-2)$ ---
that is, from quadratic to \emph{linear} in the number of buses $n$. It supports
this with a symbolic operation count (its Table~1) and two log--log plots (its
Figures~1 and~2), and validates it experimentally on five test systems of 5 to
57 buses, reporting measured speed-ups of $1.13$ to $1.73$ (its Table~5 and
Figure~5).

\subsection{Objective and Contributions}
The gap between a predicted speed-up of roughly $n/2$ and a measured speed-up of
$1.13$--$1.73$ is three orders of magnitude at the paper's largest test case. The
objective of this project is to determine, from a clean-room implementation,
where that gap comes from --- and whether the method is worth using. Concretely,
this report contributes:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A from-scratch implementation of both formulations that reproduces the
      paper's 3-bus worked example entry for entry
      (Section~\ref{sec:res-worked}).
\item An independent audit of the paper's complexity analysis, which locates a
      specific counting error in its Table~1 and replaces it with an
      element-by-element model that agrees with the paper's own measurements to
      three significant figures (Sections~\ref{sec:res-flops}
      and~\ref{sec:res-sparse}).
\item A stage-resolved timing decomposition that isolates the one step the
      paper's analysis is actually about, and shows that it does speed up as
      predicted while the end-to-end solve does not
      (Sections~\ref{sec:res-table5} and~\ref{sec:res-stages}).
\item Four extensions beyond the paper's scope: scale testing to 300 buses, a
      comparison against four additional solvers, a memory study, and a
      robustness sweep to the voltage-stability boundary
      (Sections~\ref{sec:res-scale}--\ref{sec:res-robust}).
\item \textbf{PNR+}, an augmented variant of the paper's method that treats
      PV-bus reactive power as a genuine Newton unknown. It removes the
      iteration-count penalty that makes the published method slower in
      practice.
\item A chord (frozen-Jacobian) study that carries the paper's own premise to
      its conclusion and obtains a $2.85\times$ end-to-end speed-up at 300
      buses --- the largest gain measured anywhere in this project
      (Section~\ref{sec:res-chord}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}
%% ===================================================================

\subsection{Network Model and the Bus Admittance Matrix}
A network of $n$ buses and $n_\ell$ branches is described in per-unit on a
common MVA base. Each bus carries a scheduled complex injection
$S_{\mathrm{sch},k} = P_{\mathrm{sch},k} + jQ_{\mathrm{sch},k}$ (generation minus
load) and is classified as slack (voltage magnitude and angle fixed), PV
(magnitude and real power fixed) or PQ (both powers fixed). Each branch is a
$\pi$-model with series impedance $r+jx$, total line charging $b$, an
off-nominal tap ratio and a phase shift.

The bus admittance matrix is assembled branch by branch,
\begin{equation}
Y_{bus} = C^{\mathsf H} \, Y_{br} \, C + \operatorname{diag}(y_{sh}),
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the branch--bus incidence structure and $y_{sh}$ collects the bus
shunts. $Y_{bus}$ is complex, symmetric when no phase shifters are present, and
extremely sparse: its density falls from $0.76$ on the 5-bus case to $0.012$ on
the 300-bus case. Both dense (NumPy) and sparse (SciPy CSR) assemblies are
implemented and are verified against each other.

\subsection{Standard Newton--Raphson (SNR)}
The state vector is $x = [\delta_{pv \cup pq};\; |V|_{pq}]$. The mismatch is the
real and imaginary part of~\eqref{eq:power-mismatch}, restricted to the buses
where each component is scheduled, and the Newton step solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} J_1 & J_2 \\ J_3 & J_4 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \Delta\delta \\ \Delta|V| \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} \Delta P \\ \Delta Q \end{bmatrix},
\qquad x \leftarrow x + \Delta x .
\end{equation}
The sub-matrices are obtained from two complex derivative matrices
$\partial S/\partial\delta$ and $\partial S/\partial |V|$, sliced into the
reduced real system. This is the benchmark against which the paper measures
itself, and it is implemented here in two independent ways --- a vectorised
form and a literal transcription of the textbook scalar equations --- which are
asserted equal in the test suite.

\subsection{Simplified Newton--Raphson (PNR) --- the Base Paper}
The state vector is unchanged. Writing $V_k = |V_k|e^{j\delta_k}$,
$Y_{ki} = |Y_{ki}|e^{j\theta_{ki}}$ and
$S_{\mathrm{sch},k} = |S_{\mathrm{sch},k}|e^{j\phi_k}$ and splitting
\eqref{eq:current-mismatch} into real and imaginary parts gives the paper's
equations (4) and (5),
\begin{align}
G_k &= \left|\frac{S_{\mathrm{sch},k}}{V_k}\right|\cos(\delta_k-\phi_k)
       - \sum_i |Y_{ki}V_i|\cos(\theta_{ki}+\delta_i) = 0, \\
H_k &= \left|\frac{S_{\mathrm{sch},k}}{V_k}\right|\sin(\delta_k-\phi_k)
       - \sum_i |Y_{ki}V_i|\sin(\theta_{ki}+\delta_i) = 0 .
\end{align}
Differentiating gives the paper's equations (9)--(16). The off-diagonal entries
depend on one bus index only, so an entire row of a sub-matrix shares the same
trigonometric arguments, and the diagonals are closed-form rather than
accumulated row sums. The paper writes its update as $[G;H] = J\,\Delta x$
followed by $x \leftarrow x + \Delta x$, so its $J$ is $-\partial F/\partial x$;
the implementation follows that sign convention exactly, which is why it
reproduces the printed Jacobian matrices entry for entry.

\subsection{PV Buses and the Augmented Variant (PNR+)}
\label{sec:method-pv}
Equation~\eqref{eq:current-mismatch} needs a \emph{complete} complex
$S_{\mathrm{sch},k}$, but at a PV bus only $P$ is scheduled. This is the one
place where the reformulation is not cost-free, and two treatments are
implemented:

\begin{description}
\item[\texttt{paper}] (default, faithful to the base paper) $Q_k$ is
  re-evaluated from the \emph{present} voltage estimate at the start of every
  iteration using the paper's equation (22), and only the real current mismatch
  $G_k$ is enforced. The bus contributes one equation and one unknown, exactly
  as $\Delta P_k$ does in the standard method.
\item[\texttt{augmented} (PNR+)] $Q_k$ becomes a genuine Newton unknown and
  \emph{both} $G_k$ and $H_k$ are enforced, so each PV bus contributes two
  equations and two unknowns. The extra Jacobian column is
  $-\partial F_k/\partial Q_k = j/\overline{V_k}$.
\end{description}

The distinction matters more than it appears. Lagging $Q$ makes the PV buses a
successive-substitution fixed point layered on top of Newton's method, which
costs the overall iteration its quadratic rate. Section~\ref{sec:res-scale}
shows this is the single largest effect in the whole study.

\subsection{Rectangular Current Injection (RCI)}
Proposed as an extension, RCI writes the nodal current injection in rectangular
components, $I_i = \sum_k (G_{ik}+jB_{ik})(e_k+jf_k)$, and takes the mismatch
from its real and imaginary parts. The unknowns are $(e_k,f_k)$ at every
non-slack bus plus $Q_k$ at every PV bus, with residuals
\begin{align}
R^{r}_{k} &= \frac{P_k e_k + Q_k f_k}{e_k^{2}+f_k^{2}}
             - \sum_i \left(G_{ki}e_i - B_{ki}f_i\right), \\
R^{m}_{k} &= \frac{P_k f_k - Q_k e_k}{e_k^{2}+f_k^{2}}
             - \sum_i \left(B_{ki}e_i + G_{ki}f_i\right),
\end{align}
closed at PV buses by
$R^{V}_{k} = |V^{\mathrm{sch}}_k|^{2} - (e_k^{2}+f_k^{2})$. The network part of
the Jacobian is literally $\pm G$ and $\pm B$ taken straight from $Y_{bus}$ and
is therefore \emph{constant}; only the $2\times2$ diagonal blocks and the PV
columns change between iterations. The trade-off is the system dimension,
$2(n-1)+n_{pv}$ against the polar methods' $(n-1)+n_{pq}$.

\subsection{Fast Decoupled Load Flow (FDLF)}
FDLF exploits the weak coupling between $P$ and $|V|$ and between $Q$ and
$\delta$. The Jacobian is replaced by two constant, real, symmetric matrices
built from the network susceptances,
\begin{equation}
B'\,\Delta\delta = \Delta P/|V| , \qquad
B''\,\Delta|V| = \Delta Q/|V| ,
\end{equation}
factorised once before the iteration begins. Both standard variants are
implemented: \texttt{XB} drops series resistance from $B'$, \texttt{BX} from
$B''$. FDLF is the natural control for this study, because it represents the
limit of the paper's own strategy --- a Jacobian that costs nothing to rebuild
--- at the price of linear convergence.

\subsection{Gauss--Seidel (GS)}
Included as the classical baseline, GS applies the rearranged nodal equation
bus by bus in place,
\begin{equation}
V_k \leftarrow \frac{1}{Y_{kk}}
  \left[\overline{\left(\frac{S_{\mathrm{sch},k}}{V_k}\right)}
        - \sum_{i\neq k} Y_{ki}V_i\right],
\end{equation}
over-relaxed by an acceleration factor $\alpha = 1.6$. At a PV bus the reactive
injection is estimated from the present voltages and the updated voltage is
rescaled onto its magnitude set-point.

\subsection{Chord (Frozen-Jacobian) Variants}
A further extension implements \emph{chord} iterations of both NR formulations:
the Jacobian is built and LU-factorised once (or refreshed every $k$
iterations), and subsequent steps reuse the stored factors through forward and
back substitution alone. This isolates the question the base paper raises ---
how much of an iteration is really the Jacobian rebuild --- by removing that
cost entirely rather than reducing it. Section~\ref{sec:res-chord} reports the
outcome, which turns out to be the largest single result of the project.

\subsection{Operation-Counting Models}
\label{sec:method-flops}
Three independent models of per-iteration arithmetic cost are implemented.
Following the paper's own convention, a ``FLOP'' is a multiplication or a
division; additions, subtractions and transcendental evaluations are treated as
free.

\begin{description}
\item[\texttt{paper}] The counts exactly as published, reproducing Table~1 and
  Figures~1--2 of the paper.
\item[\texttt{audited}] An element-by-element recount of the \emph{same}
  formulas, granting both methods identical common-subexpression reuse.
\item[\texttt{sparse}] The audited count with the dense off-diagonal population
  $(n-1)(n-2)$ replaced by the true number of non-zeros of $Y_{bus}$.
\end{description}

\noindent With $d$ off-diagonal entries per row, the audited unit costs of
Table~\ref{tab:audit-unit} give a predicted Jacobian cost ratio
\begin{equation}
r(d) = \frac{9d+4}{5d+13},
\label{eq:ratio-degree}
\end{equation}
whose break-even point $r(d)=1$ and dense asymptote $9/5$ are central to
Section~\ref{sec:res-sparse}.

\subsection{Software Architecture}
The implementation is written from scratch in Python with NumPy and SciPy, and
is organised as a library (\texttt{src/powerflow/}) with no I/O side effects,
driven by eight standalone experiment scripts. The library separates four
concerns:

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{case.py}, \texttt{loader.py} --- a solver-agnostic
      \texttt{PowerCase} data model and a reader for MATPOWER-format
      JSON~\cite{matpower}. Every solver consumes this one object, which is what
      keeps the comparison honest: the methods differ only in the equations they
      iterate on, never in the data they see.
\item \texttt{ybus.py}, \texttt{results.py} --- admittance-matrix construction
      (dense and sparse), branch flows, and a \texttt{PowerFlowResult} carrying
      the full per-iteration history.
\item \texttt{solvers/} --- seven interchangeable solvers behind one entry
      point, \texttt{solve(case, options, ybus)}.
\item \texttt{flops.py}, \texttt{benchmark.py}, \texttt{plotting.py},
      \texttt{reporting.py} --- the cost models, the timing and memory
      harness, and figure/table output.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A caveat on the paper's Table~3.}
The paper states that its test systems were ``modified'' but never says how, and
the bus voltages printed in its Table~3 are not those of the standard IEEE
cases --- several of its 24-bus voltages sit near $0.46-j0.91$~p.u., which no
healthy transmission network produces. The unmodified library cases are
therefore used here, and the paper's Table~3 is \emph{not} reproducible number
for number; the solutions this project obtains for TC1--TC5 are tabulated in
\texttt{03\_table3\_solutions}, where the two NR formulations agree with each
other to better than $10^{-9}$~p.u.\ on every bus. Iteration counts, convergence
shapes and timing ratios \emph{are} reproducible, and they are what the paper's
claims actually rest on.

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
Unless stated otherwise, every run uses:

\begin{itemize}
\item a \textbf{flat start} ($|V| = 1$~p.u. at PQ buses, set-point elsewhere,
      all angles zero);
\item the paper's own termination test --- a \emph{maximum voltage correction}
      of $10^{-6}$~p.u., not a mismatch tolerance;
\item dense linear algebra, except where the dense/sparse comparison is the
      subject;
\item timing as the \textbf{best of} 10--25 repeats, with $Y_{bus}$
      construction excluded, so that the figure reported is the one least
      contaminated by scheduler noise.
\end{itemize}

\noindent One exception applies to the chord experiment of
Section~\ref{sec:res-chord}, which pins BLAS and LAPACK to a single thread:
on matrices of a few hundred rows a multi-threaded BLAS spends more time waking
threads than computing, which is unfair to iterations consisting only of
triangular solves. Its absolute timings are therefore not comparable with the
other tables, though the ratios within it are.

Memory is measured as the peak heap increment over one solve using
Python's tracing allocator, which counts only allocations the solver itself
makes. All measurements were taken on an AMD Ryzen~7~5800H (16~threads) under
Python~3.14.7, NumPy~2.5.3, SciPy~1.18.1, pandas~3.0.6 and
matplotlib~3.11.2~\cite{numpy,scipy,matplotlib,pandas}.

\subsection{Verification Strategy}
Correctness is established before any performance claim is made. The test suite
contains 245 tests covering four levels:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Against the paper.} The Y-bus, the mismatch vector, the full
      Jacobian and the correction vector of all three printed iterations of the
      worked example are compared entry by entry with the published values.
\item \textbf{Internal consistency.} The vectorised Jacobians are asserted equal
      to literal transcriptions of the scalar textbook equations, and sparse
      assemblies equal to dense ones.
\item \textbf{Cross-method equivalence.} Every converged solver must zero the
      \emph{power} mismatch regardless of the residual it iterated on, respect
      the slack and PV set-points, and agree with a standard-NR reference driven
      to $10^{-13}$~p.u.
\item \textbf{Behavioural.} Warm starts, iteration limits, PV\,$\to$\,PQ
      switching under generator reactive limits, and monotonicity of the error
      history.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The full suite passes.

\paragraph{Data availability.}
Every figure in this report has a companion machine-readable table of the same
name in \texttt{results/tables/}, written as CSV and Markdown. Five result
tables are too long to typeset and are referenced in place instead: the bus-by-bus
solutions of all five test systems (\texttt{03\_table3\_solutions}, 122 rows),

(\texttt{03\_convergence\_histories}) and behind the method comparison

per-iteration traces (\texttt{08\_convergence\_histories}) are likewise

\subsection{Exact Reproduction of the Worked Example}
\label{sec:res-worked}

current mismatch vector, the Jacobian and the correction vector for three
iterations. All of it reproduces.

\paragraph{The counts cannot be right as stated.}
A dense Jacobian has $O(n^2)$ entries, and the paper's own equations (9), (11),
(13) and (15) give every one of them a \emph{distinct} admittance $Y_{ki}$. Each
entry therefore needs at least one multiplication of its own, and a cost linear
in $n$ is impossible. Inspecting the arithmetic, Table~1 counts the off-diagonal
work once per matrix \emph{row} rather than once per \emph{entry}: that is
exactly what collapses the $(n-1)(n-2)$ off-diagonal population into $(n-2)$.

\paragraph{An honest recount.}
To test whether the paper's \emph{conclusion} survives its arithmetic, the same
formulas were recounted element by element with identical common-subexpression
reuse granted to both methods. For an off-diagonal pair $(k,i)$ the standard
method must form $u = |V_i||Y_{ki}|$, $w = |V_k||Y_{ki}|$ and $m = |V_k|u$ and
then multiply by $\sin\alpha$ and $\cos\alpha$ with
$\alpha = \theta_{ki}+\delta_i-\delta_k$, filling all four sub-matrices and
their row sums: nine multiplications. The simplified summand carries neither the
$|V_k|$ factor nor $-\delta_k$, so only $u$ is needed and the count falls to
five. Against that, the simplified diagonals are closed-form expressions rather
than row sums the standard method accumulates for free, and cost thirteen
multiplications per bus against four. Table~\ref{tab:audit-unit} collects the
unit costs.

\subsection{Extension 5: Amortising the Factorisation}
\label{sec:res-chord}
Section~\ref{sec:res-stages} showed that the majority of a Newton iteration is
work the base paper's reformulation cannot touch. That observation suggests a
different attack on the same cost: instead of making the Jacobian \emph{cheaper
to build}, do not rebuild it at all. The \emph{chord} variants build and
LU-factorise the Jacobian once at the flat start and then reuse the stored
factors through forward and back substitution for every subsequent step. Both
formulations are treated this way, giving \texttt{SNR-chord} and
\texttt{PNR-chord}.

\paragraph{The cost profile inverts with system size.}
Table~\ref{tab:step-costs} times each stage of a single iteration in isolation.
The result reframes the whole study. On the small systems the paper works with,
building the Jacobian really is the dominant cost --- $60$--$64\%$ of an
iteration at 5 and 6 buses, against $6\%$ for the factorisation. By 300 buses
the proportions have reversed: the build is $30\%$ and the LU factorisation is
$\mathbf{67\%}$. The base paper therefore optimises precisely the stage whose
relative importance \emph{shrinks} as systems grow, and leaves untouched the one
that comes to dominate. Reducing a $30\%$ stage by the audited factor of $1.6$
removes about $11\%$ of an iteration; eliminating the $67\%$ stage removes
$67\%$.

arithmetic --- but it rises monotonically with size from 30 buses upward
($1.40$, $1.56$, $1.79$, $2.85$), which is the opposite of the trend for the
paper's reformulation. Figure~\ref{fig:chord-speedup} plots it, and
Figures~\ref{fig:chord-iters} and~\ref{fig:chord-time} give the underlying
iteration counts and times.

\paragraph{The strategy works on the standard formulation, not on the paper's.}
\texttt{SNR-chord} converges on every system tested. \texttt{PNR-chord} does
not: it fails outright on the 300-bus case, and on the 24-bus system --- the most
PV-dense in the set, with 10 of 24 buses voltage-controlled --- its linear
convergence rate degrades to $0.785$, requiring \textbf{57} iterations and
making it $3.3\times$ \emph{slower} than ordinary PNR. The explanation is the
one that has recurred throughout this report. Freezing the Jacobian already
reduces the method to a linear iteration; the paper's lagged-$Q$ treatment is a
second linear iteration layered on top, and the two contraction factors compound.
Measured chord convergence rates make this explicit: $0.24$--$0.40$ for
\texttt{SNR-chord} on the large systems against $0.57$--$0.79$ for
\texttt{PNR-chord}.

\paragraph{What this says about the base paper.}
The paper's premise --- that the Jacobian rebuild is the cost worth attacking ---
is correct, and this experiment confirms it from the opposite direction. Its
chosen remedy is simply far too conservative. Making the rebuild $1.6\times$
cheaper saves about $11\%$ of an iteration at 300 buses; skipping the rebuild and
the factorisation together saves $97\%$ of one. The strategy the paper points
toward is sound; carried to its conclusion it yields $2.85\times$ rather than
$1.0\times$, and it does so on the standard formulation that the paper set out
to improve upon.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
Three limitations should be stated. First, timings come from a reference
implementation in Python and NumPy; absolute numbers would differ in a compiled solver, though the
\emph{ratios} --- which is what every claim here rests on --- are far more
stable, and the stage decomposition of Table~\ref{tab:stages} isolates the
arithmetic from interpreter overhead. Second, the paper's test systems were
modified in an unstated way, so its Table~3 and its exact iteration counts are
not strictly comparable with ours; the comparison is made on the unmodified
library cases and this is flagged wherever it matters. Third, the 57-bus
derivative-ratio outlier noted in Section~\ref{sec:res-stages} is reported as
measured and not explained away, and the 57-bus loading limit is a property of
that network rather than of any solver.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
%% ===================================================================

\subsection{Key Takeaways}
This project set out to determine why the base paper's predicted speed-up of
roughly $n/2$ appears in its own experiments as a factor of $1.13$--$1.73$. The
answer has three parts, and each is supported by an independent line of
evidence.

\paragraph{1. The implementation is faithful.}
Everything the paper prints and that can be checked, checks out: the Y-bus to
four decimals, the first Jacobian to every printed digit, the mismatch and
correction vectors through three iterations, and the converged solution of
Table~2 (Section~\ref{sec:res-worked}). The disagreements reported later are
therefore disagreements with the paper's analysis, not artefacts of a different
model.

\paragraph{2. The paper's complexity analysis is wrong, but its conclusion is
right.}
Table~1 of the paper counts off-diagonal Jacobian work once per matrix row
rather than once per entry, which is what makes the cost appear linear in $n$. A
dense Jacobian cannot be filled in linear time, because each of its $O(n^2)$
entries carries a distinct $Y_{ki}$. An element-by-element recount gives both
methods a quadratic cost separated by a constant factor approaching
$9/5 = 1.80$ --- and predicts $1.733$ at 57 buses against the $1.728$ the paper
\emph{measured}. The corrected model explains the paper's own data to three
significant figures; the published model overestimates it by a factor of fifteen
(Section~\ref{sec:res-flops}).

\paragraph{3. The saving is real but structurally confined, and is more than
cancelled by a flaw in the PV handling.}
Three independent measurements converge on this. The derivative stage alone
speeds up by $1.21$--$1.60\times$, rising with system size exactly as predicted
(Tables~\ref{tab:stages} and~\ref{tab:deriv-ratio}). But that stage is only a
quarter of an iteration; $51$--$74\%$ of the work is block assembly and LU
factorisation, which are identical in the two formulations, so the
whole-iteration ratio is unity to within noise. And the paper's treatment of
PV-bus reactive power --- re-estimating $Q$ from the current voltage guess
instead of solving for it --- layers a successive-substitution fixed point on
top of Newton's method and costs it its quadratic convergence. The effect is
small on the paper's own test systems and severe beyond them: 13 iterations
against 5 at 300 buses, a fitted iteration-growth exponent six times that of the
standard method, convergence traces that are visibly straight rather than curved
on a semi-log plot, and a loss of almost half the usable loading range on the
118-bus system (Sections~\ref{sec:res-scale}--\ref{sec:res-robust}).

\paragraph{4. The defect is fixable.}
\textbf{PNR+}, which makes $Q$ a genuine Newton unknown at PV buses, restores an
almost-flat iteration count (exponent $0.101$ against PNR's $0.251$), stays
within one iteration of standard NR on every system tested, and is
$1.77\times$ faster than the published method at 300 buses. RCI, a different
current-mismatch formulation that never lags $Q$, shows the same healthy
behaviour. The deficiency therefore belongs to one specific approximation, not
to current-mismatch power flow in general.

\paragraph{5. The paper's premise is right; its remedy is too conservative.}
Freezing the Jacobian and reusing its LU factors --- the limit of the strategy
the base paper pursues --- gives a $2.85\times$ end-to-end speed-up at 300 buses,
the largest gain measured in this study, using a single factorisation instead of
five. Stage timing explains why: at that size the factorisation is $67\%$ of an
iteration and the Jacobian build the paper optimises only $30\%$, so the
published remedy can recover at most about $11\%$ of an iteration while the
chord strategy recovers $97\%$. That the chord variant works on the
\emph{standard} formulation but diverges on the paper's --- whose lagged-$Q$
fixed point compounds with the frozen Jacobian's own linear rate --- is the final
confirmation that the PV treatment, not the current-mismatch idea, is what
limits the method (Section~\ref{sec:res-chord}).

\paragraph{Practical recommendation.}
On a dense implementation of a small, well-conditioned, PV-light network, the
paper's method is a modest and genuine improvement. On anything that resembles a
modern application --- sparse storage, hundreds of buses, many voltage-controlled
generators, stressed operating points --- it should be adopted only with the
augmented PV handling, and even then the larger levers are sparse linear algebra
(a $2\times$ gain at 300 buses and growing, Table~\ref{tab:dense-sparse}) and
amortising the factorisation --- whether through FDLF-BX, the fastest solver at
118 buses (Table~\ref{tab:methods}), or through chord iterations, the fastest at
300 (Table~\ref{tab:chord}).

\subsection{Limitations}
The study is confined to balanced, positive-sequence, single-phase-equivalent
transmission networks up to 300 buses, solved from a flat start with a
reference Python implementation. Generator reactive limits are implemented but
disabled in the benchmarks, so that every method solves exactly the same problem.
Timings reflect NumPy/SciPy performance rather than that of a compiled
production solver, and the dense/sparse crossover in particular is a property of
SciPy's sparse LU and would move in another library.

\subsection{Future Work}
Four directions follow naturally from the results above.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Adaptive Jacobian refresh.} The chord study of
      Section~\ref{sec:res-chord} refreshes the factorisation never; Newton
      refreshes it every step. Neither is optimal. A policy that refreshes when
      the observed contraction rate deteriorates past a threshold should
      dominate both, and would also rescue \texttt{PNR-chord} on the PV-dense
      cases where its rate degrades to $0.785$. The solver already accepts a
      \texttt{refresh\_every} parameter, so the experiment is a sweep away.
\item \textbf{Sparse-native Jacobian assembly.} The present implementation
      assembles dense derivative matrices and converts. Building directly on the
      $Y_{bus}$ sparsity pattern would move the dense/sparse crossover well below
      300 buses and let the audited sparse model of
      Table~\ref{tab:sparse-flops} be validated against the clock.
\item \textbf{Larger and more stressed systems.} Extending to 1354-, 2869- and
      9241-bus European cases, and to continuation power flow near the nose
      point, would test whether PNR's loss of robustness worsens as predicted.
\item \textbf{A formal convergence analysis of the lagged-$Q$ iteration.}
      This report establishes the loss of quadratic convergence empirically.
      Deriving the contraction factor of the outer fixed point as a function of
      the PV-bus count and network strength would turn an observation into a
      prediction, and would say in advance which networks the published method
      is safe on.
\end{enumerate}
